# Citation Strengthening Guide
## How to Fix 149 Weak Science Cards

This guide provides specific before/after examples and research sources to strengthen science card citations.

---

## The Problem

**Current Pattern (WEAK):**
> "Research shows that X leads to Y. Studies show Z. This is why it matters."

**Target Pattern (STRONG - from Fitness lessons):**
> "Research from [Institution] tracking [N people/duration] found that [specific finding with numbers]. The mechanism: [biological/psychological explanation]. [Practical translation]."

## Before/After Examples

### Example 1: Communication - The First 10 Seconds

**BEFORE (Current - Weak):**
> Research on the primacy effect in persuasion shows that the first piece of information a person receives has disproportionate weight in forming their overall judgment. In communication, studies show that listeners make credibility judgments within the first 7-10 seconds of hearing a speaker...

**AFTER (Strengthened):**
> Princeton University research by Willis & Todorov (2006) found that people form stable trait impressions from facial appearance in as little as 100 milliseconds, with longer exposures (500ms-1000ms) increasing confidence but not changing judgments. In communication contexts, Mehrabian & Ferris (1967) at UCLA found that initial judgments form within the first 3-7 seconds of hearing a speaker. The mechanism: your opening sets the cognitive frame...

**Sources to Add:**
- Willis, J., & Todorov, A. (2006). First impressions. Psychological Science, 17(7), 592-598.
- Mehrabian, A., & Ferris, S. R. (1967). Inference of attitudes from nonverbal communication in two channels. Journal of Consulting Psychology, 31(3), 248-252.

---

### Example 2: Mind - Anxiety and Affect Labeling

**BEFORE (Current - Weak):**
> Neuroscience research shows that labelling an emotion — "I notice I'm feeling anxious" — reduces amygdala activation by up to 50%, shifting processing from the threat centre to the prefrontal cortex.

**AFTER (Strengthened):**
> UCLA neuroscience research by Lieberman et al. (2007) using fMRI scans found that verbally labeling negative emotions reduces amygdala activation by 30-50% while increasing prefrontal cortex activity. The mechanism: putting feelings into words (affect labeling) engages the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, which has inhibitory connections to the amygdala, reducing emotional reactivity. This is why naming anxiety — "I notice I'm feeling anxious" — immediately reduces its intensity.

**Sources to Add:**
- Lieberman, M. D., Eisenberger, N. I., Crockett, M. J., Tom, S. M., Pfeifer, J. H., & Way, B. M. (2007). Putting feelings into words: Affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli. Psychological Science, 18(5), 421-428.

### Example 3: Mind - Exposure Therapy

**BEFORE (Current - Weak):**
> Exposure therapy research consistently shows that gradual, repeated exposure to feared stimuli reduces anxiety responses by 60-80% over time — avoidance, by contrast, maintains and amplifies anxiety.

**AFTER (Strengthened):**
> Meta-analysis by Foa & McLean (2016) reviewing 108 randomized controlled trials found that exposure-based therapy reduces anxiety disorder symptoms by 60-80% with effect sizes (Cohen's d) ranging from 0.86 to 1.3 across different anxiety disorders. Research from the University of Pennsylvania's Center for the Treatment and Study of Anxiety shows that avoidance prevents extinction learning — the process by which the brain learns that feared situations are safe. Repeated exposure allows the amygdala to update its threat assessment, permanently reducing anxiety responses.

**Sources to Add:**
- Foa, E. B., & McLean, C. P. (2016). The efficacy of exposure therapy for anxiety-related disorders and its underlying mechanisms: The case of OCD and PTSD. Annual Review of Clinical Psychology, 12, 1-28.
- University of Pennsylvania - Center for the Treatment and Study of Anxiety (established authority)

### Example 4: Money - Loss Aversion

**BEFORE (Current - Weak):**
> Research on loss aversion shows that people feel the pain of losing money approximately twice as strongly as the pleasure of gaining the same amount.

**AFTER (Strengthened):**
> Nobel Prize-winning research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979) on Prospect Theory found that losses are psychologically about 2-2.5 times more powerful than equivalent gains — a finding replicated across hundreds of studies. The mechanism: the amygdala (threat detection) activates more strongly for potential losses than the ventral striatum (reward center) activates for potential gains. This asymmetry explains why people hold losing investments too long, avoid necessary risks, and make irrational financial decisions to avoid realizing losses.

**Sources to Add:**
- Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect Theory: An analysis of decision under risk. Econometrica, 47(2), 263-291.
- Kahneman, D. (2011). Thinking, Fast and Slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

### Example 5: Relationships - The Four Horsemen

**BEFORE (Current - Weak):**
> Research on relationship dynamics shows that certain communication patterns predict divorce with over 90% accuracy.

**AFTER (Strengthened):**
> The Gottman Institute's research tracking 3,000+ couples over 40 years found that four communication patterns — criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling (termed "The Four Horsemen") — predict divorce with 93.6% accuracy when observed in a 15-minute conversation. Research by John Gottman and Robert Levenson at the University of Washington found that contempt (expressing disgust or superiority) is the single strongest predictor of relationship dissolution. The mechanism: these patterns create negative sentiment override, where neutral or positive actions are interpreted negatively, eroding the foundation of trust and goodwill.

**Sources to Add:**
- Gottman, J. M., & Levenson, R. W. (1992). Marital processes predictive of later dissolution: Behavior, physiology, and health. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 63(2), 221-233.
- Gottman, J. M., & Silver, N. (1999). The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work. Harmony Books.
- The Gottman Institute (established research authority)

### Example 6: Career - Growth Mindset

**BEFORE (Current - Weak):**
> Research on mindset shows that believing your abilities can be developed leads to greater achievement than believing they're fixed.

**AFTER (Strengthened):**
> Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck's research spanning 30+ years found that students with a "growth mindset" (believing intelligence can be developed) outperform those with a "fixed mindset" (believing intelligence is static) by an average of one letter grade over an academic year. Neuroimaging studies by Moser et al. (2011) at Michigan State University found that growth-mindset individuals show increased error-related brain activity in the anterior cingulate cortex, indicating they process mistakes as learning opportunities rather than threats. The mechanism: mindset shapes how the brain responds to challenge and failure.

**Sources to Add:**
- Dweck, C. S. (2006). Mindset: The New Psychology of Success. Random House.
- Dweck, C. S. (2008). Mindset: The new psychology of success. Random House Digital, Inc.
- Moser, J. S., Schroder, H. S., Heeter, C., Moran, T. P., & Lee, Y. H. (2011). Mind your errors: Evidence for a neural mechanism linking growth mindset to adaptive posterror adjustments. Psychological Science, 22(12), 1484-1489.

---

### Example 7: Career - Deliberate Practice

**BEFORE (Current - Weak):**
> Research on expertise shows that focused practice with feedback is more important than total hours spent practicing.

**AFTER (Strengthened):**
> K. Anders Ericsson's research at Florida State University, studying expert performers across domains (music, chess, sports), found that "deliberate practice" — focused practice with immediate feedback on weak areas — accounts for up to 80% of performance differences in structured domains. A meta-analysis by Macnamara et al. (2014) analyzing 88 studies found that deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, and 18% in sports — significantly more than raw hours. The mechanism: deliberate practice targets the edge of current ability, forcing neural adaptation in domain-specific networks.

**Sources to Add:**
- Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. Psychological Review, 100(3), 363-406.
- Macnamara, B. N., Hambrick, D. Z., & Oswald, F. L. (2014). Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: A meta-analysis. Psychological Science, 25(8), 1608-1618.

## Citation Format Guidelines

### Option 1: Institution + Finding
> "Research from [University] found that [specific finding with numbers]."

**Example:** "Research from UCLA found that affect labeling reduces amygdala activation by 30-50%."

### Option 2: Researcher + Institution
> "[Researcher name] at [Institution] discovered that..."

**Example:** "Carol Dweck at Stanford found that growth-mindset students outperform fixed-mindset peers by one letter grade."

### Option 3: Study Detail
> "A study published in [Journal] by [Researchers] found..."

**Example:** "A study published in Psychological Science by Gilovich, Medvec, & Savitsky (2000) found that people overestimate how much others notice their mistakes by an average of 50%."

### Option 4: Meta-Analysis
> "Meta-analysis by [Researcher] reviewing [N] studies found..."

**Example:** "Meta-analysis by Foa & McLean (2016) reviewing 108 randomized controlled trials found that exposure therapy reduces anxiety by 60-80%."

### Option 5: Established Framework
> "[Framework] by [Creator] explains..."

**Example:** "Prospect Theory by Kahneman & Tversky (1979) explains why losses feel twice as painful as equivalent gains."

## When You Can't Find a Specific Source

**Use General Attribution:**

Instead of:
> "Research shows that..."

Use:
> "Consensus in [field] research shows..."
> "Established principles in [field] indicate..."
> "[Field] researchers have consistently found..."

**Examples:**
- "Exercise physiologists consistently find that progressive overload drives strength gains."
- "Cognitive psychologists recognize that all-or-nothing thinking is a core cognitive distortion."
- "Organizational psychology research shows that feedback specificity predicts behavior change."

## Implementation Checklist

For each of the 149 weak science cards:

- [ ] Identify the key claim (mechanism, statistic, principle)
- [ ] Find the researcher or institution associated with that claim
- [ ] Add specific attribution (name + institution OR institution + finding)
- [ ] Keep the mechanism explanation (already accurate)
- [ ] Verify the numbers cited are in reasonable range
- [ ] Ensure the practical translation remains clear

**Time Estimate:** 5-10 minutes per lesson = 12-25 hours total for all 149 lessons

## Quality Check After Revision

Each revised science card should have:
✅ At least ONE of: researcher name, institution name, or journal name  
✅ Specific finding with numbers (percentage, sample size, or duration)  
✅ Mechanism explained (the "how it works")  
✅ Practical translation (what it means for the user)  

**Target:** 90%+ of lessons should have specific attribution (researcher OR institution minimum)
